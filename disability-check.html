The short version

What’s in the way

SSDI actually lets you try working without losing everything. But the rules live in agency PDFs written in legalese. So people guess, and fear wins.

What I found

It’s not motivation. 48 out of 100 people on SSDI want to work. 9 actually do. The gap is fear of losing healthcare.

What I built

Plain-language rules for SSDI only. WCAG 2.2 AA from page one. And a tracker that shows where you stand before you hit a limit.

Where it is now

It’s live and I’m collecting feedback from people on SSDI. Too early to measure impact, so I’m not claiming any.

The rules that let you try work exist. Almost nobody can read them.

SSDI has real protections for people who want to work: a nine-month Trial Work Period, a three-year safety net after it, and Medicare that keeps going for years. But they're spread across agency PDFs and written in terms like "substantial gainful activity."

So people guess. They hear a story about someone who lost everything, and they decide it isn't worth the risk. For a lot of them, the check and the insurance are what keep them alive.

Fear and bad information

One wrong story from a friend or a forum can keep someone out of work for years.

Government language

The terms are legal and the thresholds change every year. Many readers have cognitive or vision disabilities.

Rules that change by state

Medicaid buy-in programs and state supplements differ at every state line, so general advice is often wrong.

The safety net is bigger than people think.

9months

You can earn any amount and keep your full check. The months don't have to be in a row.

36months

After the trial, your check comes back automatically in any month you earn under the limit.

7.5years

How long you can keep Medicare after your cash benefit stops.

Sources: SSA Ticket to Work, Trial Work Period 2026 (opens in new tab) · CBPP (opens in new tab)

Everyone who uses this site is disabled, so accessibility is the product.

WCAG 2.2 AA

I designed every page to meet WCAG 2.2 Level AA from the start. People reading it may use a screen reader, a keyboard, magnification, or none of these on a bad day. The site has to work for all of them.

Always know where you stand

The scariest part of working on SSDI is not knowing how close you are to a limit. So the site doesn't just explain the rules. It lets you track your own trial work months and earnings, and shows your status in one place, so nothing comes as a surprise.

The hardest decision was leaving SSI out.

Most people think of SSI and SSDI as one program. They aren't. SSDI is insurance you paid into through work, and its rules are based on earnings thresholds. SSI is based on need, with its own income and asset limits. Its work rules don't line up with SSDI's at all.

Putting both programs on one page would have meant adding exceptions to almost every sentence. That's the kind of confusing advice I was trying to fix. So I built for SSDI only, and kept the explanations clear and specific.

It still bothers me, because SSI recipients need this too, maybe more. If I add them, they'll get their own flow and their own plain-language rewrite.
